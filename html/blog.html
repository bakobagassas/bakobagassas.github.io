Finishing the Semester on My Own Terms

Final exams are the one real risk to my milestone. Here is my plan for Thanksgiving break, what success looks like to me beyond story points, and where my contributions stand right now.

tech reflections

Looking ahead to the rest of my semester, the only specific risk I can identify that could prevent me from completing my milestone for this class on time and at the quality I intended is the final exams. I have a lot going on as I'm trying to wrap up this semester. I have exams for other classes, projects, papers, as well as commitments from volunteering and extracurricular activities. This is the type of moment when I simply have to choose and prioritize what to do and what to set aside, and it is very easy to get carried away and forget to make contributions and reviews, because it is work that has to be done independently. Therefore, this is the moment when I have to be very organized and make sure I actually do what I'm supposed to do: not just do it, but do it intentionally and properly.

Currently, my plan is to take advantage of the Thanksgiving break. I am planning on dividing my time so that when I wake up in the morning, I study for three hours for any exam or paper that is upcoming; then I go for lunch and hang out with my family, and then in the afternoon I work on any issues I have in my queue for two to three hours depending on the amount of work I have to do, then I spend one hour reviewing the work of my classmates who are depending on me. In the evening, I spend some more time with my family, read, and work on internship applications.

It is true that contributing to open source is indeed a responsibility because even though we don't directly interact with the clients in this class, whatever product we build is still being used by them. So what helps me prioritize this work is knowing that if I work on it, it will last a long time. It is something that will be useful to the community, which I am consistently trying to give back to because it gave me a lot, so I will for sure make sure to leave a proper contribution to the repositories I contribute to by the end of this class.

I am also making sure that I document my work because next year or even years later, when I look back at my journey and how my open-source contributions all started and evolved, I want to have something to look back at and see how far I have come and see the contributions that I have made toward communities that matter to me. Therefore, by the end of this semester, I can clearly say that I have succeeded if my contributions were so high-quality that they were either merged or approved, and if I got at least 20 story points in the issues that I have solved, because currently I am trying to wrap up a lot of issues that I started previously and my contribution line is starting to look better because a lot of issues are finally getting merged and approved.

However, success is not just about my contributions to repositories; I also want to have developed, or at least enhanced, soft skills such as collaboration and communication, as they are required to work effectively in open source. So if, by the end of this semester, I get feedback that I have indeed displayed these characteristics, it would lead me to consider this semester successful. I also measure success by how satisfied my team is with my interactions with them and how satisfied my professor and TA are with my work, because they are the people that I work with the most closely, and having high story points does not mean anything if the people that I worked with are not satisfied with how we interacted together.

I have completed 7 story points for merged issues, and if I count the story points approved but not merged, it would be 12. I am planning to spend more time, as I mentioned previously, to wrap up this semester, which I estimate will lead to 20, or at least 18, story points by the end of this semester. I feel like I could have contributed even more, to be honest, but my first few months of this semester were a little overwhelming because I had too many commitments and was trying to please everyone at the same time. However, I learned my lesson, and I will make sure to have a more balanced semester next spring.

With the most recent issue I wrapped up, testing took longer than I initially anticipated because it required me to get a different notification for admins and faculty. It did take me some time, but it ended up being fine! I wrote a well-detailed pull request description, and I made sure to go through the testing procedure with the reviewers so that the steps are clear for them and that they don't have to get lost in the process, and in the end it turned out well: they were able to test it, and one of the maintainers was also able to test it and then merge the pull request.

My other contributions, apart from the issues I have been working on, were mainly about reviewing and creating new issues whenever I could. Sometimes I will take those new issues when I'm lacking any new issues, and sometimes I will just leave them so that my classmates could also have issues to work on. I think that is an important contribution because we needed to have more (relevant) issues in the repository so we could make contributions. I also did my best to provide reviews on my classmates' pull requests as much as I could, even when they are not in my assigned group, and I tried to reply to questions when I actually had an answer, for example, in the discussion I had with Alpha when he was trying to figure out the database issues that he was having while working on the Excel sheet import issue. I am quite proud of the progress that I have made in the class since the last blog post. I am catching up on things and finally getting back on track to end this semester in a way I would be proud of.

Communication, Code Reviews, and Taking Initiative

One month left in the semester. Here are three things the Open Source class reinforced in me, how I am adjusting my prioritization, and what I have been up to in the CAS repository.

tech reflections

Time flies! When I think about it, we are not even halfway done; we only have a month left in this semester. So far, my journey as a software engineer has been very interesting, both in and out of class. However, in this post, I am going to focus on my experience in the Open Source class I mentioned in my last post. Three of my beliefs/skills were reinforced in this class.

First, when it comes to collaboration, this class further reinforced my belief that communication is crucial for good collaboration. The only reason I was able to collaborate effectively with my classmates on pull requests and software development is that I managed to communicate effectively with them both in real life in the class and on GitHub.

Secondly, I realized that I very much enjoy code reviews. My labor position is probably why I realized that at first, but this class further reinforced it. It is becoming my favorite aspect of software development, as well as creating new issues on the application. And finally, I was able to be autodidactic and take on issues without being told, or go into the app, play around, and find new issues to solve by me and by my teammates. I think if there is one thing I did really well this semester, it's taking initiative without being told to.

I want to say I initially did not have a rigid plan for this class or for my semester in general. The plan I initially had was to always review any plan and adjust to fit whatever comes my way throughout this semester. So for this class in particular, I thought I would be able to spend even more time than required on open-source software. It is true that I contributed a lot to reviews, issue resolution, and creating new issues. However, I think my contribution could be even greater. I am just adjusting to a couple of new changes this semester, so I am trying to give myself some grace.

However, I still want to slightly change my strategy for the second half of this semester, mainly when it comes to my prioritization list. I usually prioritize work for classes I have to attend the following day. However, I think I can still give some attention to classes that are due in two days, because sometimes tasks and assignments feel less overwhelming when I do them in chunks rather than when they are due. So that is one thing that I am planning to change. I will still prioritize the class that is coming up the following day; however, I will set aside some time to do a chunk of work that is not due for days. I am sure this will make things better for me, and if it doesn't, then I will just revisit my plan and adapt it again.

Finally, regarding my technical contributions to open-source software, I have had a PR approved in the CAS repository since my last blog post. It was a new repository, so I spent some time setting everything up. It took me some time because the README was out of date. So I had to figure out how to get CAS running. Which in the end worked out well. I think one of the reasons I was able to run it successfully was the experience I gained from setting up BCSR. CAS is a very interesting application; it has many more features than BCSR, so I am looking forward to solving more issues and digging more into the code.

I am very excited about the new plans that we came up with in the class, especially the weekly call meetings and our spring planning. We plan to have two sprints for this semester, and I cannot wait to see everything my classmates will accomplish by the end of this class.

My Early Experiences with Open Source

I first heard the term open source on a trip to Red Hat, and then realized I had been using it for years. Halfway through my Open Source Software Development course, here are the three questions I keep asking.

tech reflections

The first time I heard of “Open Source” was during a visit to Red Hat as part of the Berea Student Software Development Team Internship trip. I was unfamiliar with the concept and didn't realize I had been an Open Source user since I started college, and perhaps even before. As a computer science major at Berea College, most, if not all, of the books I have used are open-source books from Runestone. Faculty members, as well as so many other students, contributed to those books, and they have genuinely made my student life easier because I did not have to purchase books for those specific classes

Now that I am enrolled in the Open Source Software Development (OSSD) course at Berea College, I am getting to reflect and interact more intentionally with open source Softwares, not just as a user, but also as a contributor and a student continuously learning what it actually means. I have been asking myself three main questions since I joined this class.

First: what makes software open software? Is it simply because anyone who gets to contribute can? Or is it simply because anyone who wants to use that software can freely use it however they want? In the first half of my journey in the OSSD class, I realized that the answer to this question has more layers to it. Anyone who wants to contribute to OSS can, but only under certain conditions. They have to be intentional about the code they push in; for example, it needs to be high quality, and they should not knowingly introduce security flaws. Furthermore, although everyone can technically contribute, it is still at the maintainer’s discretion whether to accept that contribution. Open source is an actual community. When you want to contribute to a project, you will be communicating with at least one person: the maintainer, the reviewers, and other contributors in the Pull Request comments, Issue Queue comments, etc. This aspect of OSS made it feel more real to me as a contributor.

On the users' side, though, Open Source does not mean people can use the software however they wish. In this class, I learned more about something called licenses. From our class discussions, I understood that licenses are a legal way to communicate to the community how they can use, distribute, or even modify code. Some licenses are more restrictive than others, but they ALL share one thing in common: you have to respect what the license implies. Which, in my opinion, is a great motivation to develop Softwares because developers are sure that their code will be used as intended; they have more control, so they feel safe producing it.

My second question was: if anyone can contribute to open-source software, then to what extent is that software secure? Since everyone has access to the codebase, they can more easily identify all the security flaws. However, through class discussions with my classmates and professors, I realized that identifying security flaws is not as bad as it initially seems. The only way of correcting something is to first KNOW that it exists. If our software is proprietary, our not noticing the security flaws does not suddenly mean they are gone. Beyond the security aspect, open source has the advantage of diverse volunteers from all backgrounds and identities working on it, which, in my opinion, increases innovation in how issues are solved within the repository because some contributors may notice things differently.

Which brings me to the last main question I had about open source softwares. Why would a firm want to make software for free? And why would someone want to give out free labor in this economy? I learned that Open source does not always mean free. If the owner of the code decides to sell their product, they are legally allowed to do so, and the licenses they use may also allow others to modify their software and use it for commercial purposes if they wish. However, what about contributors who are not allowed to use the software for commercial purposes? What incentives do they have to volunteer and work for free? This was the easiest question I could answer because I am one of those people.

An important aspect of this class is the contributions we make toward open-source software. One could argue that I receive credit at the end of the course; hence, I am not volunteering. However, I still consider this volunteering because I could have ‘easily’ chosen another class where I would not have to contribute to softwares. I chose this class not just because I was curious to learn more about open source, but, most importantly, because I was excited to contribute to more projects used by real people. Projects that genuinely bring a positive impact in people’s lives. That knowledge would have been enough for me to contribute to open source beyond just a class requirement.

So far, I have been working on 2 issues in the Berea College Syllabus Repository through this class: one where my work was merged and another where the work is still ongoing. I will talk about the one that is still ongoing because it is the most challenging and interesting one. It has been taking longer because it involves implementing a feature that lets you search for a course by its name, not just the prefix and course number. At first glance, one might think this is a straightforward issue; however, it has more layers, similar to the answers to the questions I had throughout this class. We did not use to collect course names when users added a course to the repository. Therefore, I have to take that into consideration and be more intentional about how this new feature would fit into the existing code. Which brings me to an important tool we have been using in this class: story points. Story points have been a way for us to take all this into consideration and estimate how much work it would take to solve our issues. However, life does not always go as planned, and we might underestimate or overestimate the work required to close an issue. In my case, I underestimated the work it would take to solve this issue because, when assigning the 5 story points to my issue, I only accounted for the complexity and edge cases I needed to consider. There was one important aspect I underestimated: testing. I will need to not just make sure my changes look good in the UI, but also write test cases for every new function I write and every existing function I modify, which will take more time than expected.

This case is one of the reasons why our professor introduced the ‘Delta story point' system, which I think is a great tool! It enables us to document how much our story point estimate has changed from when we picked up an issue to when it finally gets merged. In my opinion, it is important to be able to look back and access our initial opinion on the work. The delta story point is helping me notice things I didn't notice before when assessing my work, so I can use it in the future when I need to assess it again. It helps me be a better learner by improving my analysis abilities. We are only halfway through this class, and I cannot wait to see the developer I will become by the end of it.
